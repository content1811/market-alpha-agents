# Research Summary: Risk Management & Strategy Validation for a Local Multi-Agent Retail Trading System (as of August 2026)

## 1. Stop-loss placement techniques

**ATR-multiple stops.** Average True Range (Wilder's volatility measure) is the standard way to set a stop that scales with an instrument's current volatility rather than an arbitrary price distance. Common practice is a stop at entry ± (1.5–3×) ATR(14), with tighter multiples (1.5–2×) for scalping/day trades and wider multiples (2.5–3×) for swing trades to avoid being stopped out by normal noise. This is well-established technical-analysis practice (concept confirmed via general TA references; Investopedia's ATR page, normally a canonical source, was unreachable during this session — `https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/atr.asp`).

**Structure-based stops.** Placed beyond a chart structure level (swing low/high, support/resistance, prior consolidation range, or a moving average) rather than a fixed distance — the logic being that if price breaches the structure, the trade thesis is invalidated regardless of how much "room" that gives the trade. Best practice is often to combine the two: use structure to decide *where* invalidation occurs, then use ATR to sanity-check that the resulting stop distance is not abnormally tight/wide versus current volatility, and size the position (see §2) so that distance corresponds to the intended dollar risk.

**Percentage stops.** A fixed % move (e.g., 2%, 5%, 8%) is the simplest method but ignores the instrument's actual volatility regime — the same 5% stop is "tight" for a low-volatility large-cap and "loose" for a volatile small-cap or altcoin. It's most defensible as a hard backstop/max-loss cap layered on top of an ATR or structure stop, not as the primary method.

## 2. Position sizing for a ~$650 / ¥100,000 account

**Fixed fractional risk (the retail baseline).** Risk a fixed % of current equity per trade — commonly 0.5–2% for discretionary/manual retail trading, often pushed toward the low end (0.25–1%) for small, single-instrument-concentrated accounts. Position size = (equity × risk%) / (entry price − stop price). At $650 with 1% risk, that's $6.50 of risk per trade — which immediately collides with real-world frictions (see below).

**Kelly criterion and fractional Kelly.** Kelly's formula, f = (p/l) − (q/g) (fraction of bankroll to bet given win probability p, loss probability q, and win/loss fractions g/l), maximizes long-run geometric growth but requires *accurate* estimates of edge and payoff — which a live trading system never has with confidence. Because of parameter/estimation uncertainty, practitioners near-universally use fractional Kelly (½-Kelly, ¼-Kelly, or less), trading slower compound growth for much lower variance and drawdown/ruin risk (Wikipedia, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion`). For a system this early-stage and this small, fractional Kelly (≤¼) capped by a fixed-fractional ceiling (e.g., never risk more than 1% regardless of what Kelly says) is the sane default — full Kelly on noisy retail edge estimates is a recipe for blow-up.

**Volatility-adjusted sizing.** Size inversely to instrument volatility (e.g., position size ∝ 1/ATR, or target a constant "ATR-dollars-at-risk" per trade across instruments) so a volatile crypto asset and a calm large-cap contribute comparable risk to the portfolio. This is the natural pairing with ATR-based stops in §1 and is a standard feature of institutional risk-parity-style sizing scaled down for retail use.

**Practical reality check for $650/¥100,000.** This is the single most important, concrete constraint the design must confront:
- Broker/exchange minimum order sizes, lot sizes, and fees can consume a large fraction of a $6.50–$13 (1–2%) risk budget per trade, especially for US equities (no fractional-share stop-loss risk sizing on some brokers) and especially for TSE stocks.
- **TSE-specific constraint:** Japan Exchange Group unified the standard trading unit ("tangen kabu") to 100 shares for virtually all TSE-listed stocks (effective ~2018, still standard practice; JPX trading-rules pages: `https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/equities/trading/domestic/index.html`). A single unit of many liquid names (e.g., ~¥3,000/share stocks) costs ¥300,000+ — three times the entire target account. A ¥100,000 account is effectively locked out of full-unit trading in most large-cap TSE names and must rely on broker "single-share"/mini-kabu fractional services (e.g., SBI, Rakuten, Monex odd-lot programs) or restrict itself to low-priced TSE stocks, which materially narrows the tradable universe and should be modeled explicitly, not assumed away.
- **US equities PDT constraint has changed in 2026 — verify before relying on old assumptions.** The FINRA Pattern Day Trader rule (which required $25,000 equity in a margin account to make 4+ day trades in 5 business days) was **eliminated effective June 4, 2026**, replaced by an intraday-margin (IML)-based framework under FINRA Rule 4210, with a much lower standard margin-account equity floor (~$2,000) (confirmed directly from FINRA's current Rule 4210 text, `https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/4210`, which no longer contains "pattern day trader" language but does define "IML"/"intraday margin level"; corroborated independently by a Yahoo Finance piece on a trading book titled "Options Trading for the End of the Pattern Day Trader Rule," `https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/options/articles/spiking-press-publishes-wall-came-133300881.html`, and a 24/7 Wall St. piece reporting Webull management crediting "the June 4 elimination of the Pattern Day Trader Rule" with driving record retail trading volume, `https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/08/19/live-will-webull-crush-q2-earnings-tonight-after-rising-7-5-today/`). This is genuinely new for 2026 — a $650 US-equities day-trading account is no longer blocked by the old $25k/4-trades rule the way it would have been a year ago, though a $2,000 margin-equity floor and each broker's own house rules still apply, and a small account will often still need to trade a cash (non-margin) account subject to settled-funds constraints (T+1 settlement).
- Crypto has no analogous minimum-account regulatory barrier, making it the most mechanically accessible asset class for a $650 account — but with correspondingly higher volatility and thinner liquidity in many pairs on free-tier data feeds.

## 3. Holding period and profit-target estimation

**R-multiples (Van Tharp framework).** Define "1R" as the dollar amount risked on a trade (entry−stop distance × size); express every trade outcome as a multiple of R (e.g., "+2.3R," "−1R"). This normalizes performance across instruments/position sizes and is the standard way retail/prop systems evaluate expectancy (Van Tharp's position-sizing/R-multiple work is widely referenced in trading education; a direct Van Tharp Institute page was unreachable this session but the concept is standard and uncontested in the literature).

**Measured moves.** Classical technical-analysis price-projection method: measure the size of a prior impulse leg or chart pattern (flag, head-and-shoulders, range) and project an equal distance from the breakout point to estimate a target — useful as one input to a target, not a standalone edge.

**ATR-based targets.** Symmetric or asymmetric multiples of ATR from entry (e.g., stop at 1.5×ATR, target at 3×ATR for a 2:1 planned R:R) tie the target to current volatility the same way ATR stops do, and pair naturally with the R-multiple framework above (target expressed as "2R," "3R," etc., where R itself was volatility-derived).

**Holding period.** For swing systems, a common practical approach is a rule combining (a) hard time-stop (exit if target/stop not hit within N bars/days) to bound capital lock-up and psychological drift, plus (b) trailing-stop logic (e.g., trail by 1×ATR once trade reaches +1R) to let winners run without fully discretionary judgment calls.

## 5. Walk-forward validation and overfitting avoidance

Standard methodology (general quant-finance practice; specific tutorial sites like quantifiedstrategies.com and robusttrader.com were bot-blocked/unreachable this session, so this is stated from established methodology rather than a single fresh source):
- **Walk-forward optimization**: split history into sequential in-sample (fit/optimize parameters) and out-of-sample (test, no re-fitting) windows, roll forward through time, and only trust performance aggregated across the *out-of-sample* segments — never the in-sample-optimized numbers.
- **Anchored vs. rolling windows**: anchored keeps a growing in-sample window from a fixed start; rolling keeps a fixed-length in-sample window that slides forward — rolling is generally preferred for markets suspected of regime change (crypto, and increasingly equities).

A directly relevant and very current (Aug 2026) piece of literature synthesizes the modern statistical toolkit for exactly this problem: **"Equity Strategy Backtesting: Luck or Edge? The MinervaScore as a Statistical Robustness Grade"** (arXiv:2608.23808, Aug 2026, `https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23808`), which combines four established validation quantities as the current best-practice checklist:
1. **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)** — corrects a backtest's Sharpe ratio for the number of strategy variants/parameters trialed (the more you tried, the more your best-looking backtest is expected to be lucky, not skillful) — directly addresses multiple-testing bias endemic to any agent-driven signal-generation system that tries many indicator combinations.
2. **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)** — estimates the likelihood that the strategy selected as "best" in-sample will underperform out-of-sample.
3. **Superior Predictive Ability (SPA) test** (White's Reality Check / Hansen's SPA) — a formal hypothesis test for whether a strategy beats a benchmark after accounting for search over many candidates.
4. **Minimum Track Record Length (MinTRL)** — how much real out-of-sample history is needed before a claimed Sharpe ratio is statistically distinguishable from zero/luck at a given confidence level.

Notably, this same 2026 paper reports that in a **pre-registered out-of-sample test on real market data**, its composite robustness score showed **no significant forward relationship with future performance** (Spearman ρ=0.013, p=0.40) in a population of backtests with "limited surviving edge" — i.e., even a sophisticated 2026 statistical-robustness layer could not reliably identify future winners among real submitted backtests. That is a sobering, timely, and directly relevant data point for this project: rigorous validation reduces the odds of deploying a purely-lucky backtest, but it is *not* a guarantee of future edge.

A second relevant, current paper specifically on the crypto/RL side: **"Deep Reinforcement Learning for Cryptocurrency Trading: Practical Approach to Address Backtest Overfitting"** (arXiv:2209.05559, updated through 2023, `https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559`) — frames overfitting detection as a hypothesis test and shows less-overfit agents outperform more-overfit ones and naive benchmarks over a real 2022 crash period, reinforcing that overfitting-aware selection helps but the achieved edge over passive benchmarks is described modestly, not as a guaranteed money-maker.

**Practical minimum bar for this project**: before trusting any agent-generated signal with real capital, require (a) walk-forward out-of-sample testing, not just a single in-sample backtest; (b) an explicit accounting of how many parameter/rule variants were tried (to sanity-check DSR/PBO informally even without full statistical apparatus); (c) a live paper-trading/forward-test period of at least several weeks to months before committing real money, since backtests cannot capture real slippage, latency, or free-tier data gaps.

## 6. Key performance metrics

- **Sharpe ratio** = (mean return − risk-free rate) / std. dev. of returns, annualized. Penalizes upside and downside volatility equally.
- **Sortino ratio** = (mean return − target) / downside deviation only — more appropriate for strategies with asymmetric (e.g., trend-following) return distributions since it doesn't punish big wins (Wikipedia, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortino_ratio`).
- **Win rate** is meaningless alone — must be paired with average win/loss size (R-multiple); a 30% win rate can be strongly profitable at 4:1 reward:risk, and a 70% win rate can lose money at 1:3.
- **Profit factor** = gross profits / gross losses; >1 profitable, commonly ~1.5–2+ considered "good" for a retail systematic strategy, but very high figures in a backtest are a red flag for overfitting/survivorship contamination rather than a reason for confidence.
- **Max drawdown** — largest peak-to-trough equity decline; usually paired with CAGR as the **Calmar ratio** to judge whether returns compensate for pain endured, and is arguably more decision-relevant for a manual trader than Sharpe, since a human executing trades manually will abandon a system after a large drawdown regardless of its long-run Sharpe.

**Survivorship bias** is a distinct and important failure mode for backtests: using only currently-listed/currently-existing symbols (current S&P 500 members, currently-trading crypto tokens) inflates historical performance because delisted/failed/rug-pulled instruments are silently excluded. Academic estimates put this bias at roughly **0.9%/year** for US mutual fund studies (Elton, Gruber & Blake, 1996, cited via Wikipedia `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias`) — and the effect is plausibly larger and more dangerous in crypto, where a meaningful fraction of small-cap tokens from any given period no longer exist or no longer trade.

## 7. Honest, non-hype assessment: is 100,000 JPY → 150,000+ JPY (50%+) via day/swing trading realistic?

**Short answer: as a near-term, reliably-expected outcome, no — treat it as an aspirational stretch goal with a small probability of success and a real probability of losing a meaningful fraction of the capital, not as a plan to bank on.**

Evidence and reasoning:

- **Retail day trading is, on average, a losing activity.** The most rigorous evidence comes from full-population brokerage/exchange datasets, not surveys: a study of essentially all individual day traders in the Brazilian futures market found **97% of those who persisted more than 300 days lost money**, with only ~1.1% earning more than Brazil's minimum wage from trading (Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, "Day Trading for a Living?", SSRN 3423101, referenced via `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_trading`, `https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101`). A parallel long-running research line using the complete Taiwan stock exchange dataset (Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean and related papers, e.g. SSRN 529063) reaches similarly discouraging conclusions about day-trader profitability net of costs. The SEC and FINRA's own investor-education material explicitly warns prospective day traders to expect the possibility of severe losses. These are full-population, survivorship-bias-free datasets — the "10% success rate, 1% real money" figures circulating on trading-education sites are less rigorous but point the same direction.
- **A 50%+ return target compressed into "near-term" is a return profile associated with high-variance, high-risk-of-ruin strategies**, not with validated, risk-controlled systematic trading. Well-run systematic retail/prop strategies that survive multiple years typically target **annualized Sharpe ratios in the 1–2 range** and CAGRs more in the range of 15–40%/year with real cost/slippage drag — not 50% in a short window. Backtests claiming Sharpe >3 or 50%+ returns over short periods should be treated as *likely artifacts of overfitting/survivorship/lucky sampling* until they survive the kind of DSR/PBO/walk-forward scrutiny in §5 and, ideally, a live forward-test.
- **Account size compounds the difficulty, it doesn't help it.** At $650/¥100,000: (a) fixed costs (spreads, commissions, slippage, and on TSE the 100-share unit constraint) consume a disproportionate share of a 1–2% per-trade risk budget; (b) the sample of trades achievable in "near-term" is small, so realized results will be dominated by variance/luck rather than converging to any true edge; (c) any single string of a handful of losing trades (entirely normal even for a good strategy) can be a large percentage hit to such a small base, and psychologically pressures a human operator toward the exact position-size creep and revenge-trading behavior that most reliably destroys small accounts.
- **Known failure modes that will bite an unvalidated automated signal system specifically:**
  - *Overfitting/curve-fitting* — an agent system iterating over many indicator/parameter combinations will, almost by construction, find combinations that looked great historically by chance; §5's DSR/PBO/walk-forward discipline is the direct countermeasure, and even then isn't a guarantee (per the MinervaScore paper's own out-of-sample null result).
  - *Slippage and execution friction* — free-tier data (often delayed, lower-resolution, or gapped) plus manual (human-in-the-loop) execution means real fills will be worse than backtest fills assume, especially in fast-moving crypto or around TSE auction sessions.
  - *Regime change* — a strategy fit to 2023–2026 data (unusually calm equity markets punctuated by sharp AI-driven rotations, and a maturing-but-still-volatile crypto market) can fail abruptly when volatility regime, correlation structure, or liquidity conditions shift; this is exactly what walk-forward/rolling-window testing is meant to probe for, but can't fully insure against.
  - *Survivorship bias* in any backtest universe that isn't point-in-time correct (using today's index/exchange membership list applied to past dates) — a real risk if the local backtesting setup pulls "current constituents" rather than historical listings, especially for crypto token universes where failed/delisted tokens vanish from most free data sources.

**What realistic performance looks like for a well-built retail systematic strategy, stated plainly:** a small but genuine edge (Sharpe ~0.5–1.5 after realistic costs), most of the "return" coming from disciplined risk management (small consistent losses, occasionally larger wins) rather than a high win rate, meaningful drawdown periods (20–40%+ peak-to-trough is common even for decent systems) that must be survived without abandoning the process, and a multi-month-to-multi-year validation runway (paper trading + small real capital) before the operator has statistically meaningful evidence the edge is real rather than noise. Framed against the project's own aspirational target: growing ¥100,000 by 50%+ is not impossible on a lucky run, but it is **not the expected outcome of "refining the system"** — it is closer to the tail outcome of a distribution whose median outcome, per the research above, is flat-to-negative for most systematic small-account retail traders, and whose left tail (losing a large fraction of the ¥100,000) is at least as probable as the right tail the target describes. The system should be designed, and success criteria for the project should be framed, around capital preservation and demonstrated statistically-validated edge first, with the 50% figure treated explicitly as an unlikely stretch outcome rather than a plan.
